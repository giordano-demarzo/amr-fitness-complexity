Resistance doesn't spread at random.

When a bacterium learns to defeat one drug, it becomes far more likely to defeat related drugs next. We mapped 20 years of global surveillance into a co‑resistance network — and used it to forecast each pathogen's next move.

Pick a pathogen and watch its resistance ignite across the network, year by year.

How the atlas works

1

Map the resistome

We turn 20 years of Pfizer ATLAS / Vivli surveillance into a network of antibiotics that tend to fail together across bacterial species, using tools built to study economic complexity.

2

Resistance is contagious along the network

A pathogen resistant to a drug's network neighbours is dramatically more likely to acquire that drug next. In a leakage‑free back‑test (2020–2024) this network signal beats the naïve "how common is it" baseline — reaching an AUC of 0.87 for the most predictable "contagion‑hub" antibiotics.

3

Fitness & complexity that mean something

Pathogen fitness and antibiotic complexity recover known biology: WHO priority ESKAPE pathogens rank as fittest, and last‑resort "Reserve" drugs rank as most complex (ρ = 0.59).

4

Forecast the next move

For every pathogen still susceptible to a drug, we score its network exposure and calibrate it into a probability of acquiring resistance next — a watch‑list for the drugs each bug is poised to defeat.

Research prototype built from aggregated Pfizer ATLAS surveillance data (governed by the Vivli data‑use agreement); reference labels from the WHO AWaRe 2023 classification and the ESKAPEE priority list. Forecasts are statistical signals from historical co‑resistance patterns, not clinical guidance.
